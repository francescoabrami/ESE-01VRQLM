\subsection{Introduction}
\label{introlab02}

Operational amplifiers are fundamental analog electronic devices widely used for signal amplification and conditioning. This laboratory investigates the behavior of inverting and non-inverting amplifier configurations using a AU741 op-amp and compares experimental measurements with the ideal theoretical model.

\subsubsection*{Objective}

The objective of this laboratory is to analyze the performance of feedback operational amplifier circuits. Gain, input and output resistance, bandwidth, and frequency response are experimentally measured using an oscilloscope and signal generator. Bode plots are obtained for both the non-inverting and audio amplifier configurations. The measured results are compared with theoretical predictions and the limitations of the LM741 operational amplifier, such as its gain-bandwidth product and slew rate, are evaluated.

\subsubsection*{Virtual ground}

Before moving on, for these additional parts, the input signal was set back to a 1 kHz sinusoidal wave with 1 $V_{pp}$.

We now examine both input terminals of the operational amplifier: $V^+$
  and $V^-$. In an ideal op-amp, these two terminals are at the same voltage. Since $V^+$ is connected to ground, we would expect both inputs to read 0 V.
  
  However, when measuring $V^+$, we observe a very noisy signal with a peak amplitude of about 17.6 mV and an average value of approximately 540 $\mu$V. This is extremely small and can be considered close to zero, with the observed fluctuations attributable to noise and measurement limitations.
  
  At the $V^{-}$ terminal, we observe a very small, noisy sinusoidal signal. This behavior arises because, in a real differential amplifier, the input-output relationship can be approximated as:
  
\begin{equation}
	V_{out} = A \cdot(V^+ - V^-)
\end{equation}

which can be rearranged as:

\begin{equation}
	V^- = V^+ - \frac{V_{out}}{A}
\end{equation}
  
  Given that the open-loop gain $A \approx$ 2 $\cdot$ $10^5$, the voltage difference between the inputs is theoretically expected to be extremely small, about 200000 times smaller than $V{out}$, and 180$^\circ$ out of phase with the output signal.
  
  As previously noted, we include a table summarizing the measured values. Since the non-inverting input ($V^+$) is tied to ground, it represents the reference potential. The inverting input ($V^-$) behaves as a virtual ground due to the negative feedback mechanism, meaning it is held very close to 0 V. In practice, both terminals are not exactly equal, but the difference is extremely small and only observable as noise and residual error.

As a first operation we can calculate the $R_{seen}$ which is the resistance seen by the voltage source. Please not that is not the $R_{in}$ of the op-amp we want to calculate.
We get that:

\begin{equation}
	R_{seen} = R_3 \cdot \frac{V_{in,S3\;open}}{V_{in,S3\;closed} - V_{in,S3\;open}} = 9952.94 \pm 2150 \; \Omega 
\end{equation}

This, as we said is not the resistance $R_{in}$ of the op-amp, but it the result of the voltage divider between the $R_{in}$ and $R_{10k}$ which is the bias resistor of the circuit.

Since we expect a very large $R_{in}$ (2 M$\Omega$ from data sheets) it is clearly visible that the $R_{10k}$ is biasing our result.
We can undo the voltage divider and calculate the value of $R_{in}$. This operation will introduce a lot of uncertainties given the division with two number of the same order of magnitude. As we can see the $\delta$ for $R_{seen}$ is already considerable thus we will not propagate it even further.

\begin{equation}
	R_{in} = \frac{R_{seen} \cdot R_{10k} }{R_{seen} - R_{10k}} = \frac{9952.94 \cdot 10000}{10000 - 9952.94} = 2114946.88 \approx 2.1 \unit{M}\Omega
\end{equation}

To measure the $R_{out}$ we need to use the difference in the $V_{out}$ value caused by the insertion of the load $R_5$.\\

As a first operation we can estimate the output resistance of closed loop (CL) $R_{out,CL}$.

\begin{equation}
	R_{out,CL} = R_3 \cdot \frac{V_{out,S7\;closed} - V_{out,S7\;open}}{V_{out,S7\;open}} = 18.033 \; \Omega 
\end{equation} 

As we can see this value differs from the one reported on the data sheets. This is due to the feedback loop. In the calculated case the feedback network (outside the IC) was configured with a 100 k$\Omega$ resistance $R_1$ between the output and the inverting input and a 12 k$\Omega$ resistance $R_2$ between the inverting input and ground.\\

Given these information we can account for the close loop that usually affects (via the feedback) the value of the output resistance bringing it down.
By not going too deep in this matter we can say that:

\begin{equation}
	R_{out,closed} \approx \frac{R_{out,open} }{1 + A\beta}
\end{equation}

By using $R_{out,closed}$ $\approx$ 18 $\Omega$ and $R_{out,open}$ = 75 $\Omega$ we get that:

\begin{equation}
	1 + A\beta \approx \frac{75}{18} \approx 4.17
\end{equation}

So we get a loop gain of approximately 3.17 that is a reasonable result for this frequency and setup.
This result is only to have an idea on how well our calculations and operations that we have done are somewhat correct.\\

As we can see the Bode diagram is pretty \textit{"Boring"} which is very good for this case. Specifically we can see that the amplifier in this conditions (is not open-loop) can amplify very well, with almost a 20 dB gain every signal from low frequency all the way up to around 100 kHz.\\

This limit is given by the feedback resistance, here are the calculations.

\begin{equation}
	A_v = \frac{R_f}{R_{in}} + 1 = 9.333
\end{equation}

We can then convert it to dB, we get that:

\begin{equation}
	G_{max} = 20 \cdot \log_{10} \left ( A_v \right ) \approx 19.4 \unit{dB}
\end{equation}

With this known we can also predict the expected bandwidth from $GBW$:

\begin{equation}
	f_{BW} \approx \frac{GBW}{A_v} = \frac{1 \unit{MHz}}{9.333} \approx 107.15 \unit{kHz}
\end{equation}

In this case, differently from before, some other component were introduced changing the behavior of the amplifier.
Specifically adding the $C_f$ in the feedback network added a high cut-off frequency of around 10.6 kHz. As we can see from the theoretical Bode diagram also a cut-off region is present on the low frequency side. This behavior is due to the series of the components $R_{in}$ and $C_{in}$ that create a cut-off before the frequency of around 133 Hz. Between the two region a flat gain region with a value of 18.4 dB is present.\\

Here are the calculations that led us to those theoretical result.\\

We start from the close loop gain of the circuit:

\begin{equation}
	A_v = \frac{R_f}{R_{in}} + 1 = 9.333
\end{equation}

We can then convert it to dB, we get that:

\begin{equation}
	G_{max} = 20 \cdot \log_{10} \left ( A_v \right ) \approx 19.4 \unit{dB}
\end{equation}

Now we can calculate the frequency where the high side pole will be located.
To do this we consider the feedback network.
Knowing that $R_f$ and $C_f$ are 100 k$\Omega$ and 150 pF we get that:

\begin{equation}
	f_{C,high} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_f C_f} \approx 10.6 \unit{kHz}
\end{equation}

We don't have to forget that at the input there is also an high pass filter given by the series of the components $R_{in}$ and $C_{in}$.

\begin{equation}
	f_{C,low} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{in} C_{in}} \approx 132.6 \unit{Hz}
\end{equation}

With this we can draw the theoretical Bode diagram which will be flat between $f_{C,low}$ and $f_{C,high}$ with a gain of around 18.4 dB. Also our calculations match the expected $f_{BW}$, since:

\begin{equation}
	f_{BW} \approx f_{C,high} - f_{C,low}
\end{equation} 

For the domain of frequencies that are outside the $f_{BW}$ domain will be attenuated by 20dB/dec.\\

As seen in the Bode diagram shown in \autoref{audioampbode} our predictions are compatible with the data we gathered. As seen for the previous case the amplifier has a better response better than the one we predicted for frequencies greater than $f_{C,high}$.

Regarding the lower frequency side we can see slightly worts performances that would not be ideal for an audio amplifier. This is due to the $f_{C,low}$ being too high respect the $\approx$ 20 Hz of the lower frequency that the human body can ear. For example if someone who likes techno or dance music would rather not use this device.\\

\subsubsection*{DC gain}

Before coming to and end we can stimate the DC Gain of the audio amplifier we just analyzed.\\

We are going to do this by measuring the average values of the input and output voltages and then add a $\pm$ 500 mV offset and observe what changes at the output. Here are the data we gathered the signal frequency of the sinusoidal was set to 5 kHz and the $V_{pp}$ to 1.

Give these results we can say that since the audio amplifier is AC-coupled due to the input coupling capacitor and the capacitor in the feedback network. 

These capacitors block the DC component of the input signal, resulting in a DC gain close to zero. Consequently, changing the input offset does not affect the steady-state output voltage, and only the AC component of the signal is amplified.
Hence the value we see (2.90 V and 2.92 V) are the same and given by the $V_{avg,in}$ being amplified and not by the others offsets.
