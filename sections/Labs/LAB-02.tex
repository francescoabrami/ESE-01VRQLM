\subsubsection*{Virtual ground}

Before moving on, for these additional parts, the input signal was set back to a 1 kHz sinusoidal wave with 1 $V_{pp}$.

We now examine both input terminals of the operational amplifier: $V^+$
  and $V^-$. In an ideal op-amp, these two terminals are at the same voltage. Since $V^+$ is connected to ground, we would expect both inputs to read 0 V.
  
  However, when measuring $V^+$, we observe a very noisy signal with a peak amplitude of about 17.6 mV and an average value of approximately 540 $\mu$V. This is extremely small and can be considered close to zero, with the observed fluctuations attributable to noise and measurement limitations.
  
  At the $V^{-}$ terminal, we observe a very small, noisy sinusoidal signal. This behavior arises because, in a real differential amplifier, the input-output relationship can be approximated as:
  
\begin{equation}
	V_{out} = A \cdot(V^+ - V^-)
\end{equation}

which can be rearranged as:

\begin{equation}
	V^- = V^+ - \frac{V_{out}}{A}
\end{equation}
  
  Given that the open-loop gain $A \approx$ 2 $\cdot$ $10^5$, the voltage difference between the inputs is theoretically expected to be extremely small, about 200000 times smaller than $V{out}$, and 180$^\circ$ out of phase with the output signal.
  
  As previously noted, we include a table summarizing the measured values. Since the non-inverting input ($V^+$) is tied to ground, it represents the reference potential. The inverting input ($V^-$) behaves as a virtual ground due to the negative feedback mechanism, meaning it is held very close to 0 V. In practice, both terminals are not exactly equal, but the difference is extremely small and only observable as noise and residual error.

\begin{table}[h!]

\hline

\hline
$V_{in,S3\;closed}$  &  1.06 & $\pm$ 0.048 & $\pm$ 04.52 \% &  V \\ 
\hline
$V_{in,S3\;open}$  &  0.720 & $\pm$ 0.048 & $\pm$ 06.67 \% &  V \\
\hline
$R_{3}$  &  4700 & $\pm$ 235 & $\pm$ 5 \% & $\Omega$ \\
\hline
$R_{10k}$  & 10000 & $\pm$ 500 & $\pm$ 5 \% & $\Omega$ \\

\end{tabular}
\caption*{Input and output of non-inverting op-amp}
\end{table}

As a first operation we can calculate the $R_{seen}$ which is the resistance seen by the voltage source. Please not that is not the $R_{in}$ of the op-amp we want to calculate.
We get that:

\begin{equation}
	R_{seen} = R_3 \cdot \frac{V_{in,S3\;open}}{V_{in,S3\;closed} - V_{in,S3\;open}} = 9952.94 \pm 2150 \; \Omega 
\end{equation}

This, as we said is not the resistance $R_{in}$ of the op-amp, but it the result of the voltage divider between the $R_{in}$ and $R_{10k}$ which is the bias resistor of the circuit.

Since we expect a very large $R_{in}$ (2 M$\Omega$ from data sheets) it is clearly visible that the $R_{10k}$ is biasing our result.
We can undo the voltage divider and calculate the value of $R_{in}$. This operation will introduce a lot of uncertainties given the division with two number of the same order of magnitude. As we can see the $\delta$ for $R_{seen}$ is already considerable thus we will not propagate it even further.

\begin{equation}
	R_{in} = \frac{R_{seen} \cdot R_{10k} }{R_{seen} - R_{10k}} = \frac{9952.94 \cdot 10000}{10000 - 9952.94} = 2114946.88 \approx 2.1 \unit{M}\Omega
\end{equation} 

\subsubsection*{Output resistance}

As we have come to a result for the input resistance we can now and go and estimate the output resistance $R_{out}$.
As indicated by the laboratory paper a 1 kHz sinusoidal 1 $V_{pp}$ signal was applied to the source and the input and output of the op-amp were probed.
Now the setup no longer includes the $R_3$ resistance but it has been added a $R_5$ fo 2.2 k$\Omega$ to act as a load.
Here are reported the data that we measured.


\begin{table}[h!]
\centering	

\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\textbf{Object} & \textbf{Measure}   & \textbf{$\delta$} & \textbf{$\varepsilon_{\%}$} & \textbf{Unit} \\

\hline
$V_{in,J3}$  &  1.06 & $\pm$ 0.048 & $\pm$ 04.52 \% &  V \\ 
\hline
$V_{out,S7\;closed}$  &  9.84 & $\pm$ 0.480 & $\pm$ 04.88 \% &  V \\ 
\hline
$V_{out,S7\;open}$  &  9.76 & $\pm$ 0.480 & $\pm$ 04.91 \% &  V \\
\hline
$R_{5}$  &  4700 & $\pm$ 235 & $\pm$ 5 \% & $\Omega$ \\
\hline
$R_{out,OL}$  &  $\approx$ 75 &  - &  -  & $\Omega$ \\

\end{tabular}
\caption*{Output resistance $R_{out}$ of non-inverting op-amp}
\end{table}


To measure the $R_{out}$ we need to use the difference in the $V_{out}$ value caused by the insertion of the load $R_5$.

As a first operation we can estimate the output resistance of closed loop (CL) $R_{out,CL}$.

\begin{equation}
	R_{out,CL} = R_3 \cdot \frac{V_{out,S7\;closed} - V_{out,S7\;open}}{V_{out,S7\;open}} = 18.033 \; \Omega 
\end{equation} 

As we can see this value differs from the one reported on the data sheets. This is due to the feedback loop. In the calculated case the feedback network (outside the IC) was configured with a 100 k$\Omega$ resistance $R_1$ between the output and the inverting input and a 12 k$\Omega$ resistance $R_2$ between the inverting input and ground.\\

Given these information we can account for the close loop that usually affects (via the feedback) the value of the output resistance bringing it down.
By not going too deep in this matter we can say that:

\begin{equation}
	R_{out,closed} \approx \frac{R_{out,open} }{1 + A\beta}
\end{equation}

By using $R_{out,closed}$ $\approx$ 18 $\Omega$ and $R_{out,open}$ = 75 $\Omega$ we get that:

\begin{equation}
	1 + A\beta \approx \frac{75}{18} \approx 4.17
\end{equation}

So we get a loop gain of approximately 3.17 that is a reasonable result for this frequency and setup.
